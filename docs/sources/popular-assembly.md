# Popular Assembly

**Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_assembly

**Theme:** Collective decision-making

## Overview of Athenian Direct Democracy

Athenian democracy, emerging in the Greek city-state of Athens from 460 BCE to 320 BCE, is recognized as one of the earliest forms of direct democracy. The Athenian democracy was direct, rather than representative, meaning that Athenian men themselves made up the Assembly.

## The Assembly (Ekklesia)

The ekklesia, or Assembly, was the sovereign governing body of Athens. Any member of the demos—any one of those 40,000 adult male citizens—was welcome to attend the meetings of the ekklesia, which were held 40 times per year in a hillside auditorium west of the Acropolis called the Pnyx.

At the meetings, the ekklesia made decisions about war and foreign policy, wrote and revised laws and approved or condemned the conduct of public officials. Ostracism, in which a citizen could be expelled from the Athenian city-state for 10 years, was among the powers of the ekklesia.

## Key Democratic Institutions

The system was comprised of three separate institutions:
- **Ekklesia** — sovereign governing body that wrote laws and dictated foreign policy
- **Boule** — council of representatives from the ten Athenian tribes
- **Dikasteria** — popular courts in which citizens argued cases before lottery-selected jurors

The boule was a group of 500 men, 50 from each of ten Athenian tribes, who served on the Council for one year. Unlike the ekklesia, the boule met every day and did most of the hands-on work of governance. Positions on the boule were chosen by lot and not by election. This was because, in theory, a random lottery was more democratic than an election: pure chance, after all, could not be influenced by things like money or popularity.

## Historical Development

Solon (594 BC), Cleisthenes (508–507 BC), and Ephialtes (462 BC) all contributed to the development of Athenian democracy. It is most usual to date Athenian democracy from Cleisthenes since Solon's constitution fell and was replaced by the tyranny of Peisistratus, whereas Ephialtes revised Cleisthenes' constitution relatively peacefully.

## Limitations

The political rights were confined to adult male citizens, excluding women, enslaved individuals, and non-residents from the democratic process.
